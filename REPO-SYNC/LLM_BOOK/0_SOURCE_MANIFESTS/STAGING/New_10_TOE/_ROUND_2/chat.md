## Block E: the moves as behavior

### E1: The catalog, as tells
[frame] Ignore the subject matter of the talk. Identify every recurring reasoning operation the speaker performs. For each: (1) name it as a verb phrase of 3–5 words; (2) define the move; (3) give two examples from the talk with chapter titles; (4) describe the **physical tell**: what a person doing this move visibly does, asks, or refuses, so that a writer could show it without a speech; (5) say what goes wrong when the move is absent. Aim for 8–14 operators. Operators, not conclusions.

### E2: The washing-machine test
In the opening of the talk the speaker reads a description that sounds like process philosophy and reveals it is a machine's description of a washing machine, then lists the mistakes intellectuals make. Extract, as a step-by-step a person could perform in conversation, what the speaker does when someone dresses a plain thing in big words: the questions he asks, the substitution he tries, the test he applies, and what he refuses to do (does he refuse to mock, refuse to concede, refuse to translate back?). Then give the mirror case: what he does when a plain-sounding sentence hides a real structure. Cite chapters.

### E3: Before you bolt it on
[frame] The speaker's recurring question is whether an operation that worked in one place is licensed in another. Turn that into the checklist the older engineer would say aloud while fitting a salvaged part into a new machine: what he asks about where the part was proven, which conditions made it work there, which of those conditions the new machine lacks, what failure would look like, and whether fitting the part reveals, creates, transforms, or blocks something. Keep every item as a question a mechanic could ask with his hands on the part. Cite the chapters where the speaker performs each item.

### E4: Three ways a build goes wrong
[frame] Apply the speaker's local-to-global taxonomy to a machine built from parts that each worked elsewhere. For each of the three (unique but transformed whole; no whole possible; many wholes with nothing to choose between them), give: the mechanic's **first sign** (what he would notice with his hands or ears before he could say why), the **wrong response** the speaker warns against, and the **right response**. Keep the speaker's convention that these are candidates until shown. Then say which of the three the speaker thinks people most often mistake for the others, and why.

### E5: The fiat test, said aloud
The speaker describes four ways a problem gets "solved" and singles out solving by fiat: excluding the counterexamples by a definition that only exists to exclude them, as in calling the non-deterministic cases "unphysical". Give the test as a sentence or two the older engineer could say to someone whose claim survives only because its failures have been defined out of bounds, and the decision procedure behind it (the exclusionary label; the definition without reference to the problem; the dependency test). Then give the honest version: how a scope restriction is announced when it is legitimate, so the younger engineer can tell the two apart. Cite chapters.

### E6: The hunch, and how not to kill it
The speaker describes discovery guided by something not yet articulable: a cloud with a rock somewhere in it, pitches that get rejected, a detector that fires on the right one, and the risk that the rock is created by dredging. Give: (a) the steps as the young engineer would live them, with the physical tells (what he does when a pitch is wrong; what he does when one is right); (b) what the speaker says, or implies, about what a mentor must **not** do with another person's hunch (formalize it for him, name it early, demand the 80%); (c) the two failure modes: articulating too early and never articulating; (d) what the second speaker's seed-and-flower image adds. Cite chapters.

### E7: Slogan fatigue as a method
The speaker says he agrees with a maxim the first fifty-odd times and starts doubting it around the fifty-seventh, calls this a personality trait, and uses it anyway as a detector. Give it to the older engineer as a method: what he does when he hears a phrase he has heard a thousand times (the sign, the prophecy, the slogan), how he tells the version that still carries structure from the version that is only polish, and where the speaker admits the method misfires. Then give one way the young engineer can be right **and** sound like a slogan, and how the older one should test that case without dismissing it. Cite chapters.

### E8: Keep it simple, earned
The speaker distinguishes compression that is generative (it comes back with theorems that inform you) from compression that is decorative (it makes an ordinary idea sound profound). Give the test as something an engineer does when he simplifies a design: what has to come back from the simplification for it to be earned, what it looks like when the simplification only made the drawing prettier, and how the speaker handles the temptation to borrow heavy mathematics as ornament. Cite chapters, and say which examples are from the transcript and which from the reports.

### E9: That's not what I mean by how it works
The speaker's example of a scientist who was given a correct mechanical account of the brain and said "that's not what I mean" is a case of a thick concept being deflated. Give: the signs that a deflation has happened (what the answer sounds like, what it leaves out), what the person who notices it does next (not what he concludes), and the move the speaker calls the deflation's twin (the deflated thing inflating everything that shares the property). Then apply it to a machine: the account that explains every part and misses what the machine is for. Cite chapters.

### E10: How two engineers argue well
The talk ends in a Q&A with a second speaker who disagrees in places. From that exchange and from the speaker's own habit of moving between example, pattern, and mathematics, describe the **shape** of a good argument between two people who respect each other: who goes up a level and who pulls down, how a disagreement is held open without being dissolved into "you're both right" (which the speaker explicitly refuses), what each concedes, and what the second speaker's expressivist point does to the first speaker's position. Give it as a dialogue shape with beats, not as a transcript. Cite chapters.

### E11: Half a blueprint
The speaker is against premature unification, not against unification: a whole may be forced only when the gluing conditions are earned, and a global theory can map its obstructions as permanent features instead of forcing harmony. Apply this to two people who each hold half of a plan for a machine. Give: the signs that joining the halves is premature; what the speaker says a careful person does instead (how he maps the obstruction rather than papering it); and what it looks like when the join is earned. Then say what the watcher who refuses to build would be doing, in the speaker's terms, by refusing. Cite chapters.

### E12: The probe loads the circuit
The speaker describes consciousness as resisting formalization because inspection changes its state, and uses an averted-vision image (look slightly to the side to see a faint star). Give: what he says about investigation changing the investigated; what he recommends doing about it (how to look); where he insists the resemblance to measurement in physics is structural rather than poetic; and how an engineer who knows that a probe loads the circuit it measures would recognise the same move. Cite chapters; mark anything that is outside the sources.

### E13: Where they need each other
[frame] Using everything above and nothing outside the sources: where, in the speaker's own framework, does a young engineer with a true hunch need an older one who tests everything, and where does the older one need the younger? Where would each fail alone (name the failure in the speaker's terms)? And where does the man who refuses to build stand relative to both? Answer as five or six short paragraphs a writer could plan scenes from. Ideas only; no names.
