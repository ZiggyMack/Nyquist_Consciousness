## 1. The Rulebook of the Warrior's Way

Rule 1: Impeccability. One-Line: Impeccability is doing your best in whatever you are engaged in by making the most efficient, frugal use of your energy without waste. Book: Tales of Power (Chapters 8 & 11) & The Fire from Within (Chapters 2 & 7). Failure Guarded Against: the catastrophic loss of personal power caused by acting like an immortal being with infinite time, which leaves a practitioner helpless, drained, and vulnerable to defeat by the unknown or by petty tyrants.

Rule 2: Controlled Folly. One-Line: Controlled folly is choosing an act and performing it with total commitment as if it mattered, while fully knowing that nothing is more important than anything else. Book: A Separate Reality (Chapter 5) & Tales of Power (Chapter 11) [36–37]. Failure Guarded Against: paralysis, despair, and emotional vulnerability caused by taking oneself, one's success, or one's fellow human beings too seriously [35–37].

Rule 3: Death as an Advisor. One-Line: A warrior keeps the constant awareness of impending death at their left shoulder, using its presence as an advisor to evaluate every act as if it were their last battle on earth. Book: Journey to Ixtlan (Chapter 4) & A Separate Reality (Chapter 12) [43–44, 63]. Failure Guarded Against: indulging in pettiness, procrastination, timidity, and the delusion that one has plenty of time to change [63, 67–69].

Rule 4: Erasing Personal History. One-Line: Erasing personal history is creating a fog around oneself so that no one—not even oneself—knows with certainty who one is or what one will do. Book: Journey to Ixtlan (Chapter 2) & Tales of Power (Chapter 1) [57–60, 78]. Failure Guarded Against: being trapped, pinned down, and drained by the fixed expectations and encumbering thoughts of family, friends, and society [57–59, 78].

Rule 5: Becoming Unavailable (Inaccessible). One-Line: Becoming unavailable means touching the world around oneself sparingly and deliberately, avoiding exhausting oneself or squeezing others to nothingness. Book: Journey to Ixtlan (Chapters 6 & 7) [65–66]. Failure Guarded Against: clinging out of desperation, standing in the middle of a trafficked road waiting to be clobbered, and burning out relationships through routine and boredom [65–66].

Rule 6: Losing Self-Importance. One-Line: Losing self-importance is dethroning the belief that oneself is central or special by unmasking that importance as self-pity in disguise. Book: Journey to Ixtlan (Chapter 3) & The Fire from Within (Chapters 2 & 7). Failure Guarded Against: spending most of one's life perpetually offended, annoyed, or distracted by the deeds and misdeeds of fellow human beings.

Rule 7: "Not-Doing". One-Line: Not-doing is performing actions that break away from habitual cognitive routines ("doing") in order to interrupt the internal dialogue and stop the world. Book: Journey to Ixtlan (Chapter 15) & Tales of Power (Chapter 11). Failure Guarded Against: re-creating and locking down the same ordinary, agreed-upon description of reality day after day through uninterrupted mental chatter.

Rule 8: The Art of Stalking. One-Line: Stalking is the systematic, surreptitious control of one's own behavior using four moods—ruthlessness, cunning, patience, and sweetness—to move and fixate the assemblage point. Book: The Eagle's Gift (Chapter 14), The Fire from Within (Chapters 11 & 12), and The Power of Silence (Chapter 6) [104–105, 109, 131, 153–155]. Failure Guarded Against: uncontrolled emotional outbursts, self-deception, getting trapped by habit, and being destroyed by social interactions or petty tyrants.

## 2. How Controlled Folly Avoids Cynicism: The Books' Own Answer
In A Separate Reality (Chapter 5) and Tales of Power (Chapter 11), Castaneda explicitly challenges don Juan on this exact point, asking how a person can go on living and acting with full commitment if nothing matters, without slipping into total emptiness, apathy, or cynicism [35–37, 39]. Don Juan's answer centers on three elements:

The Choice of Will and Nature Over Intellect: In A Separate Reality (Chapter 5), cynicism or emptiness happens when a person is thinking about life rather than seeing or living it [35–36, 38]. An average person's commitment depends on believing their acts have objective importance. When "seeing" dispels that illusion, a non-warrior becomes bitter or cynical [35, 38–39]. A warrior goes on living not because acts are important, but because of tempered will and the sheer bent of their nature: "My will controls the folly of my life... I choose to live, and to laugh, not because it matters, but because that choice is the bent of my nature" [35–36].

Choosing a "Path with Heart": In A Separate Reality (Chapter 5) and Journey to Ixtlan (Chapter 9), a warrior is never a cynic because they deliberately choose a "path with heart". Because no single act is inherently more important than any other, a warrior selects acts that bring joy, peace, and laughter. They act out the chosen role with absolute gusto, passion, and "lusty efficiency," while remaining internally detached from the outcome: "His controlled folly makes him say that what he does matters and makes him act as if it did, and yet he knows that it doesn't; so when he fulfills his acts he retreats in peace, and whether his acts were good or bad, or worked or didn't, is in no way part of his concern" [36–37, 44].

Acting for the Spirit vs. Acting for Profit: In The Power of Silence (Chapter 6) and The Eagle's Gift (Chapter 14), cynicism is the product of expecting rewards, recognition, or profit. Ordinary men act for personal gain; when gain fails, they become bitter. A warrior practices controlled folly as a humble gesture to the spirit and the Eagle, acting "not for profit but for the spirit". Knowing that human affairs are utter folly in the face of the Eagle's finality, controlled folly serves as the only honorable bridge between human daily life and eternity.

## 3. Shifts Across the Years & Reference Note
In earlier books (A Separate Reality), controlled folly is presented as an individual psychological/existential stance for navigating human social interactions. In later books (The Fire from Within and The Power of Silence), controlled folly is re-conceptualized energetically as a core branch of stalking—a deliberate behavior designed to unhook the assemblage point from self-reflection.
