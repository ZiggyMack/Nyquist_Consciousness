Here is the filmable, physical sequence of the apprenticeship actions as presented across the books, stripped of personal names and narrative plot, focusing strictly on concrete actions, visual/sensory details, and the underlying mechanics.

## (1) How the Teacher Sets a Task the Apprentice Must Do Alone
Task A: The Spot on the Porch. The Teachings of Don Juan (Chapter 1) [9–12]. Action: the teacher points to an 8x12 foot porch floor and sets a riddle: the apprentice must find his single unique "spot" (sitio) where he feels naturally strong and happy, while avoiding an "enemy" spot that drains energy or causes death. Setting the Task: the teacher states that nothing is a gift and the riddle must be solved alone without further hints. He then walks around the house into the chaparral and goes inside, leaving the apprentice outdoors in total isolation overnight.
Task B: The Senseless Chores. Tales of Power (Chapter 11). Action: the teacher assigns absurd, repetitive physical routines—sweeping dirt and debris from one pile to another, arranging firewood in precise geometric patterns, encircling a house with concentric lines drawn in the dirt with a finger, or wearing a specific cap. Setting the Task: the teacher delivers these demands casually or as a joke, demanding strict execution without ever explaining their purpose, then commands the apprentice to abruptly drop the routine once established.
Task C: The Eight Days of Lying to Oneself. Journey to Ixtlan (Chapter 15). Action: for eight consecutive days, the apprentice must lie to himself deliberately: instead of repeating his habitual internal narrative that he is inadequate or ugly, he must state the exact opposite while remaining fully aware that he is lying. Setting the Task: assigned as an action to disrupt the apprentice's habit of self-pity ("doing"), forcing him to realize that both self-praise and self-blame are artificial constructs.

## (2) How Quieting the Internal Dialogue is Taught as Physical Practice
A. The Right Way of Walking. Tales of Power (Chapter 11) & Journey to Ixtlan (Chapter 3) [58, 102–103]. Hands: curl fingers loosely over the palms in unaccustomed, claw-like positions to draw physical awareness to the arms [45, 58, 102–103]. Eyes: keep eyes completely unfocused, sweeping them across an arc from the tips of the feet to just above the horizon without staring at anything, taking in a 180-degree peripheral view to flood the mind with sensory data [102–103].
B. Gazing. The Second Ring of Power (Chapter 3) & The Art of Dreaming (Chapter 1) [128–130, 293]. At What: progresses sequentially from dry leaves to small plants, trees, moving insects, rocks, rain, fog, and clouds [128–129]. Body Position & Eyes: sit on a cushion of natural fibers or dry leaves with the spine propped firmly against a tree trunk, rock, or pole. Tilt the hat brim to visor excessive light, half-close the eyelids, and scan the object very slowly in a counterclockwise direction without moving the head or focusing the gaze sharply.
C. Not-Doing. Journey to Ixtlan (Chapter 15) [76–81]. The Pebble's Shadow: place a small pebble on a boulder. Instead of looking at the pebble as a distinct object ("doing"), focus entirely on the tiny shadow cast underneath it, treating that shadow as "glue" binding the pebble to the boulder. Afterwards, dig a hole and bury the pebble as if it were a part of one's own body [77–78]. The Lines of the World: bend the elbow, curve the fingers as if gripping a door knob, and execute a circular pushing-pulling lever motion in the air until the hand feels a cold, viscous resistance ("mushiness"). When the hand stays warm, use it to feel the "lines of the world" extending through space.

## (3) What the Apprentice Feels and Sees, and the Mistakes Made
At the Porch Spot: paces for hours, feels silly; rolls on the floor, experiencing alternating cold and warm sensations. Sees peripheral greenish-yellow, purple, and verdigris color shifts at the outer edges of vision. On the "enemy" spot, feels a sudden physical pressure on the stomach, intense apprehension, rising neck hair, and involuntary clawing of the hands. Mistakes: tries to analyze the riddle rationally; attempts to feel with the skin/body instead of using peripheral vision ("feeling with the eyes"); complains of infinite possibilities; wants to quit out of frustration [9–10].
During Senseless Chores & Walking: feels an unexpected physical lightness, as if being pulled forward by the hands; perceives a massive, unanalyzed surge of environmental sound and peripheral detail [102–103]. Mistakes: demands logical explanations; treats chores as silly jokes; tries to categorize sensory data verbally rather than letting it inundate the mind [102–103].
During Gazing & Not-Doing: hand turns ice-cold while paddling air; feels stomach pressure/nausea; sees shadows take on 3D volume, transparency, and cavernous depth. Mistakes: stares directly at the object ("doing") rather than scanning its edges/shadows; tries to explain the visual distortion logically; succumbs to dizziness or self-indulgence.

## (4) What the Teacher Says When the Apprentice Asks "Why"
"Your problem is that you want to understand everything, and that is not possible" [53–54]. "If you insist on understanding you're not considering your entire lot as a human being". "Words are your predilection... Talking is doing for you". "You get caught in your own questions". "There is nothing to understand. Understanding is only a very small affair".

## (5) What the Teacher Refuses to Explain and Why
What is Refused: the exact internal mechanics of how the assemblage point shifts, how non-ordinary reality operates, or the literal meaning of perceptual phenomena [76–77, 103–104, 108]. Why: translating these experiences into words forces them back into the rational mind's internal dialogue ("doing"), which immediately restores the ordinary description of reality [76–77, 79, 108]. Instruction is directed exclusively to the body, not reason: "Who cares whether or not you understand? When one does something with people, the concern should be only with presenting the case to their bodies".

## Shifts Across the Books & Reference Note
Middle Books (Journey to Ixtlan / Tales of Power): chores, walking, gazing, and not-doing are taught as direct physical techniques to quiet thought and "stop the world" [77, 102–103]. Later Books (The Fire from Within, Chapter 7): the teacher reframes all early physical routines as deliberate "props" or decoy maneuvers designed to trick the apprentice's rational mind away from self-importance so the assemblage point could shift.
